### Notes on the definitions that are easiest to implement wrong

**Grimes's pivot orders are recursive, and the recursion is where the value is.** A first-order pivot high is any bar with a higher high than its two neighbours. Grimes is blunt that this structure is so common as to be nearly meaningless on its own — every major turn is at a pivot, but almost no pivot is a major turn, and marking every one of them on a chart makes the whole set look far more consequential than it is (`grimes_art_science` p.33). A second-order pivot high is a first-order pivot high with a *lower* first-order pivot high on each side. A third-order pivot high is a second-order pivot high with a lower second-order pivot high on each side. Third-order pivots are what he means by "the market structure" of a timeframe (`grimes_art_science` p.34). Two implementation consequences follow directly. First, an order-*n* pivot cannot be confirmed until the *next* order-*(n−1)* pivot on the far side has itself been confirmed, so higher-order pivots carry a substantial and variable confirmation lag — they always look far more significant in the middle of a chart than at the hard right edge (`grimes_art_science` p.34). Second, there is no rule forcing pivots of any order to alternate; three same-side pivots in a row are legal, and Grimes explicitly warns that systematic implementations must plan for it, taking the extreme of the run as the anchor when drawing the swing (`grimes_art_science` pp.34–35).

**Brooks's swing definition is deliberately looser, and that is a real disagreement, not a paraphrase difference.** Brooks accepts a bar whose high is *at or above* both neighbours (`brooks_ranges` p.14). On M1 and M5 gold, equal highs are common, so the two definitions produce measurably different swing sets. The v1.2 draft collapsed both into "completed extremum on stated TF", which is why its swing lists could not be reproduced. Keep two named series.

**Breakout terminology is a state machine, not a set of independent patterns.** Brooks's sequence is: breakout → the market eventually pulls back → at that moment every breakout should be regarded as failing → the pullback either reverses the market (failed breakout, trend reversal) or does not (breakout pullback, trend resumption) (`brooks_ranges` p.52). Three refinements sit on top. A *breakout test* is a breakout pullback that returns to within a few ticks of the breakout point or entry price; it may arrive on the very next bar or twenty-plus bars later, and it is the setup Brooks rates most highly for re-entry (`brooks_ranges` pp.11, 53–54). A *breakout gap* is the space between the breakout point and the shallowest point of the pullback; if the pullback never reaches the breakout point the gap survives and its midpoint often becomes the midpoint of the whole leg, giving a measured-move projection (`brooks_ranges` pp.26, 76). A *failed failure* is the third state: the fade of the failed breakout is itself reversed, converting the failed breakout retrospectively into a small flag and resuming the original direction (`brooks_ranges` p.12). Because these are states of one object, the bot must carry a single per-breakout record through the whole sequence rather than emitting independent pattern labels.

**Dalton's initiative/responsive distinction is geometric, not behavioural.** The classification depends only on where the activity occurs relative to the *previous* period's value area, not on how aggressive it looks. Buying that occurs within or above prior value is initiative; buying below prior value is responsive. Dalton is explicit that price auctioning down and finding buyers is not by itself responsive — it is the location below value that makes it so (`dalton_mind_over_markets` p.66). He also notes the mirror-image property that trips up naive implementations: a responsive buying tail and an initiative selling range extension are frequently *the same event* viewed from the two sides, so the bot must classify the participant it is joining, not the bar.

**Excess is defined by hindsight and must be treated as decaying evidence.** Dalton concedes that excess is only identifiable after it has formed, and that its usefulness rests on the assumption that the same other-timeframe participant will act again at that price (`dalton_mind_over_markets` pp.130–131). He later constrains this sharply: a spike extreme is a reliable reference only for the *first* probe back into it, because a second test within the same interval establishes acceptance inside the spike and negates it (`dalton_mind_over_markets` p.252). Any implementation that keeps excess levels at constant weight is using half of Dalton's rule.

**Timeframe nesting is definitional, not a heuristic.** Brooks states it as an identity: every pullback is a small trading range on the chart in front of you, and every trading range is a pullback on a higher timeframe (`brooks_ranges` p.82). He extends it to say that a trading range is essentially a pullback that has lasted long enough to lose its short-term predictive power, at which point most breakout attempts in either direction fail (`brooks_ranges` p.134). Grimes reaches the same place from the opposite direction: the market can genuinely be in an uptrend, a downtrend and a range simultaneously, depending only on which timeframe is being asked (`grimes_art_science` p.209). This is why every structural field in the Implementation table is keyed by timeframe and why cross-timeframe claims are forbidden without explicit translation.

## Distilled rules

1. Compute pivots by Grimes's strict rule on every timeframe you label: a pivot high needs a strictly higher high than the bar immediately before **and** immediately after it (`grimes_art_science` p.32). Because the confirming bar must close, every pivot is only known one closed bar late — never label the current bar a pivot.
2. Do not act on first-order pivots alone. Escalate to second- and third-order pivots before calling anything "structure" (`grimes_art_science` pp.33–34).
3. Allow non-alternating pivots. When several same-side pivots of one order occur in a row, anchor the swing line to the most extreme of the run (`grimes_art_science` pp.34–35).
4. Label the trend on each timeframe as `up`, `down`, or `uncertain` — never force a binary. Uncertain is a legitimate, tradable-avoidance state (`grimes_art_science` p.96).
5. Do not upgrade "failure to make a new extreme" to "trend change". Require the opposite-side pivot to be violated on a closed bar before flipping the label (`grimes_art_science` p.96).
6. When a single leg produces both a higher high and a lower low, mark the state `transition`, then require a subsequent lower high **and** a second break of the low before declaring a downtrend (`grimes_art_science` pp.96–97).
7. Before flipping the HTF trend label to `down` in an established uptrend, test whether the structure is a complex (two-legged) consolidation. If the second countertrend leg terminates near the first, treat the "trend change" as a continuation trap (`grimes_art_science` p.97).
8. Maintain an `always_in` flag on H1 and M15 at all times, including inside ranges. Flip it only after a breakout with follow-through: normally two consecutive closed strong trend bars in the same direction (`brooks_reversals` pp.328, 333–334).
9. If you cannot decide the always-in direction from the last few bars, fall back in order: majority of recent closes vs the 20-EMA, then the direction of the move that led into the current congestion. If still undecided, the market is a range — trade the edges only (`brooks_reversals` pp.329–330).
10. Do not take a countertrend setup that is too weak to flip the always-in direction. If it cannot flip the flag, it is not worth trading (`brooks_reversals` p.332).
11. Suspend always-in swing logic inside a tight range. Wait for the breakout or the failed breakout instead (`brooks_reversals` p.331).
12. Record the breakout point explicitly on every break — which price, on which timeframe, from which structure. A break is only valid if the bar that exceeded the level has **closed** beyond it on that timeframe (`brooks_ranges` p.30; `murphy_ta` p.78).
13. Treat every breakout as provisional until the first pullback resolves. The first pullback is the diagnostic event, not the breakout bar (`grimes_art_science` p.163).
14. Apply Grimes's failure-test filter: if price spends more than about three bars beyond the level without conviction, or closes decisively back inside within those bars with momentum, classify the breakout as failed (`grimes_art_science` p.147).
15. Do not treat a pullback that dips back through the breakout level as automatic proof of failure. Failure requires momentum and a decisive close, not a wick (`grimes_art_science` p.146).
16. When a fade of a failed breakout is itself reversed within a few bars, mark `failed_failure` and revert to the original breakout direction; size it as a second-entry setup, not a fresh idea (`brooks_ranges` p.12).
17. Store all levels as zones with an explicit half-width, never as scalars, and prefix every level in reasoning output with "potential" (`grimes_art_science` pp.115, 126).
18. Score level significance before using it. Require at least two of: HTF pivot (H4 or D1), three or more prior touches, an excess tail/spike extreme, prior-session high/low, or round number (`grimes_art_science` p.119; `murphy_ta` p.71). Unscored levels are decorative and must not carry a trade.
19. Never justify a trade solely by "large target, tight stop at the level". In a random walk that arrangement has zero expectancy; you need an independent reason to expect non-random behaviour at the level (`grimes_art_science` pp.119–120).
20. Require a *decisive* penetration before applying role reversal. Use an instrument-scaled filter (see Implementation), not a tick, and prefer a closing break plus a hold (`murphy_ta` pp.74, 78).
21. Prefer entries where a clean level coincides with an overextended approach (a sharp spike into it). A slow grind into a level is weak evidence for the level holding and often precedes a break (`grimes_art_science` p.126).
22. When a magnet (measured move, prior-session extreme, round number, opening-range projection) sits within reach, bias trades toward it and only consider counter-entries after it has been reached or overshot (`brooks_ranges` pp.66, 81).
23. Project a measured move from the first spike or from the range height at every breakout, and use it as the primary partial-profit reference rather than as an entry trigger (`brooks_ranges` pp.71, 76).
24. Classify each new session's open against the prior session's range and value proxy: inside value, inside range but outside value, or outside range. Inside value ⇒ expect rotation and estimate the day's span from the prior session's span; outside range ⇒ allow trend playbook (`dalton_mind_over_markets` pp.90–92, 95–96).
25. Label every entry as initiative or responsive with respect to the prior session's value area, and state it in the reasoning output (`dalton_mind_over_markets` p.66).
26. When two or more consecutive sessions show overlapping value, mark `balance = true`. Inside balance, only the edges are tradable; the middle is the worst location (`dalton_mind_over_markets` pp.159, 197).
27. On a balance breakout, go with the break and place the invalidation just back inside the balance boundary; if price re-enters and holds, exit immediately and prepare for a break of the opposite edge (`dalton_mind_over_markets` pp.202–204).
28. Treat excess (a long tail or a gap) as a reference level only while it is untested. Once price trades back into it and spends a second interval there, downgrade it (`dalton_mind_over_markets` pp.131, 252).
29. Compute the rotation factor over the session's fixed intervals as an objective directional tally, and require it to agree in sign with the always-in flag before taking a with-trend swing (`dalton_mind_over_markets` p.133).
30. Stand aside when price is chopping across an intermediate moving average with no structural break — the absence of imbalance nearly guarantees no edge (`grimes_art_science` p.209).
31. Never merge structure across timeframes without translation. A break of an M5 swing is not a break of an H4 level; state the owning timeframe on every structural claim (`brooks_ranges` p.82).
32. Before a rule is promoted above `weak`, standardise its outcomes as R-multiples and test the mean against zero; win rate and reward/risk in isolation prove nothing (`grimes_art_science` p.409).

## Worked examples

All examples are XAUUSD, decisions taken **on bar close only**, UTC session clock (Asia 00–07, London 08–13, overlap 13–16, NY 16–21). Prices are illustrative.

### 1 — Third-order pivot break on H4 (structural context)
- **Setup:** H4 has printed a rising sequence of second-order pivot lows for six swings. Current H4 bar closes below the most recent third-order pivot low at 3,318.0.
- **Decision:** WAIT on entry; change H4 regime label from `up` to `transition` and suspend H4 long bias for lower-timeframe setups.
- **Invalidation:** an H4 close back above 3,318.0 within the next two bars restores `up`.
- **Why:** a penetration of a significant third-order pivot materially changes the environment, but the labelling tool is context, not a trade trigger (`grimes_art_science` pp.35, 96).
- **Evidence:** moderate

### 2 — Complex consolidation trap (the pattern FAILS and the book explains why)
- **Setup:** H1 uptrend. Price makes a lower high at 3,362, then breaks the prior pivot low at 3,340 — a textbook Dow trend change to `down`. Two H1 bars later price stalls at 3,336, which is within a few dollars of the *first* countertrend leg's low from earlier in the day, and the two legs are of similar size.
- **Decision:** SKIP the short. Re-label the structure as a two-legged (complex) pullback in an intact H1 uptrend.
- **Invalidation:** an H1 close below 3,336 with a bear trend body, extending the second leg well past the first, restores the downtrend reading.
- **Why:** Grimes shows explicitly that a strict Dow-rule implementation flags a trend change at exactly the point you should be buying, because complex consolidations are built from two lower-timeframe countertrend legs (`grimes_art_science` p.97). This is the single most common way a mechanical HH/HL labeller loses money.
- **Evidence:** moderate

### 3 — Always-in flip on the London open
- **Setup:** Asia ranged 3,340–3,352. At 08:00 UTC the first two M15 bars close as large bull trend bars with small tails, the second closing at 3,357, above the Asia high.
- **Decision:** OPEN long context; set `always_in = long` on M15. Entry on the first M5 pullback that closes above the prior M5 bar's high, not at market on the spike.
- **Invalidation:** M15 close back below 3,352 (the Asia high, now the breakout point).
- **Why:** an always-in flip requires a spike with follow-through — normally two consecutive strong closed trend bars (`brooks_reversals` pp.333–334); entering on the pullback avoids buying where large participants are distributing into breakout buyers (`grimes_art_science` p.140).
- **Evidence:** moderate

### 4 — Breakout test that holds (breakout pullback long)
- **Setup:** Continuing from #3. Four M15 bars later price retraces to 3,352.6, printing a bull-bodied M15 bar whose low is within a dollar of the breakout point, and the retrace has lasted three bars.
- **Decision:** OPEN long on a stop above the test bar's high.
- **Invalidation:** M15 close below the low of the test bar, i.e. clearly under the breakout point.
- **Why:** a breakout pullback that returns to within a few ticks of the breakout point is a breakout test; if buyers again overwhelm sellers there, two legs up are the base case (`brooks_ranges` pp.11, 53–54).
- **Evidence:** moderate

### 5 — Failed breakout of the Asia high (fade)
- **Setup:** Pre-London M15 bar pokes 4.5 above the Asia high at 3,352 on a bar with a long upper tail. The next two M15 bars close back below 3,352, the second a bear trend bar closing near its low.
- **Decision:** OPEN short on the close of the second bar back inside, or on a stop below its low.
- **Invalidation:** M15 close back above 3,352.
- **Why:** the level was probed to reach clustered stops without follow-through; two-to-three bars outside followed by a decisive close back in with momentum is Grimes's failure-test filter (`grimes_art_science` pp.147–148), and Brooks's inertia argument says most range breakout attempts fail (`brooks_ranges` p.134).
- **Evidence:** moderate

### 6 — Failed failure: the fade in #5 is reversed
- **Setup:** After the short in #5 triggers, the very next M15 bar is a strong bull trend bar that closes above the high of the failed-breakout bar, back outside 3,352.
- **Decision:** CLOSE the short; re-enter long only on a stop above the prior bar's high, treating the sequence as a breakout pullback.
- **Invalidation:** M15 close back below 3,352 again — at which point stand aside entirely, as the level is behaving as a stop-hunting zone in both directions.
- **Why:** a failure that fails resumes in the original breakout direction and converts the failed breakout into a small flag; because it is a second signal it is treated as more reliable than the first (`brooks_ranges` p.12).
- **Evidence:** weak — Brooks asserts the reliability improvement; nothing in the corpus tests it.

### 7 — Open-Auction inside prior value (WAIT)
- **Setup:** London opens at 3,347, inside the Asia range and inside the Asia session's ~70% volume band (3,343–3,350). The first three M15 bars rotate above and below the open with overlapping bodies and prominent tails.
- **Decision:** WAIT. Do not take the first M5 directional push in either direction.
- **Invalidation of the wait:** an M15 close outside the Asia range that then holds on a retest.
- **Why:** an open inside prior value with two-sided rotation says sentiment has not changed; early extremes have a low probability of holding, and the correct plan is to let the session build its own extremes and trade those (`dalton_mind_over_markets` pp.90, 95).
- **Evidence:** moderate

### 8 — Balance-area breakout with acceptance (initiative long)
- **Setup:** Three consecutive daily sessions with heavily overlapping value bands, roughly 3,330–3,358 on D1. The NY session closes an H1 bar at 3,364, above the balance high, and the next H1 bar holds above 3,358 and closes higher.
- **Decision:** OPEN long, initiative.
- **Invalidation:** H1 close back inside 3,358 — exit at once rather than waiting for a stop far below.
- **Why:** Dalton's balance-breakout rule is to go with the break and place the protective exit a few ticks back inside the balance, because re-entry into the balance is itself the rejection signal (`dalton_mind_over_markets` pp.202–203).
- **Evidence:** moderate

### 9 — Balance breakout that reverses (SKIP the second guess, then reverse)
- **Setup:** Same balance as #8, but price breaks *below* 3,330 first and no follow-through develops; an H1 bar closes back inside within two bars.
- **Decision:** CLOSE the short at minimal loss; do NOT immediately reverse long. WAIT for a break of the *upper* balance edge and trade that instead.
- **Invalidation:** a second acceptance below 3,330 (two H1 closes below with the second lower) restores the short case.
- **Why:** Dalton describes exactly this "rock rocks one way, falls the other" sequence: a probe beyond a reference that finds no new activity licenses the opposite side to act with confidence (`dalton_mind_over_markets` pp.203–204). It also matches Brooks's breakout-mode framing — direction must not be pre-committed (`brooks_ranges` p.11).
- **Evidence:** moderate

### 10 — Responsive buy at excess below value
- **Setup:** Overlap session drives down to 3,321, well below the prior session's value band (3,343–3,350), and prints an M15 bar with a long lower tail; the following M15 bar closes above the tail bar's midpoint.
- **Decision:** OPEN long, responsive, target the lower edge of prior value (3,343) as the first magnet.
- **Invalidation:** M15 close below the tail's extreme at 3,321.
- **Why:** buying below prior value is by definition responsive activity, and the tail is day-timeframe excess — the auction went too far, found the other side, and returned (`dalton_mind_over_markets` pp.66, 130–131). The prior value edge is a magnet, so trade toward it and reassess on arrival (`brooks_ranges` p.66).
- **Evidence:** moderate

### 11 — Role reversal on H4 after a decisive break
- **Setup:** H4 resistance zone 3,368–3,372, touched four times over eight sessions. An H4 bar closes at 3,389 — roughly 0.5% beyond the zone — and two subsequent H4 bars hold above 3,372. Three sessions later price retraces into 3,373–3,370.
- **Decision:** OPEN long on an M15 confirmation close inside the zone.
- **Invalidation:** H4 close below 3,368 (the zone's lower edge).
- **Why:** role reversal applies only after a penetration significant enough to convince participants they were wrong; Murphy's benchmark is 1–3% for major levels, less for short-term ones, and the zone qualifies on time-spent, touch-count and recency (`murphy_ta` pp.71–74).
- **Evidence:** moderate — the mechanism is well described but untested; Grimes's random-level experiment applies as a direct caveat (`grimes_art_science` pp.117–119).

### 12 — Decorative level: SKIP
- **Setup:** M5 shows a "clean" horizontal at 3,355.4 formed by two M5 highs 40 minutes apart during Asia. Price returns to it during London on rising range.
- **Decision:** SKIP. Do not trade the level.
- **Invalidation:** none needed — the trade is simply not taken.
- **Why:** the level fails every significance test: it is not an HTF pivot, has two touches on the lowest timeframe, no excess, no session extreme, no round number. Grimes's experiment shows that random lines produce exactly this appearance, so its visual cleanliness is not evidence (`grimes_art_science` pp.117–119, 119).
- **Evidence:** strong — the negative claim (unqualified levels are indistinguishable from random) is the one thing in this topic backed by a reproducible test.

### 13 — Measured move as target, not trigger
- **Setup:** London prints an opening range 3,344–3,352 over the first 60 minutes, then an M15 bar closes at 3,356 above it with a large bull body.
- **Decision:** OPEN long on the first M15 breakout pullback; set the primary partial-profit level at 3,360 (opening-range height projected from the breakout point) and trail the remainder.
- **Invalidation:** M15 close back inside 3,352.
- **Why:** an opening-range breakout typically reaches about a measured move of the range height, and the range edges then serve as intraday reference levels (`brooks_reversals` pp.413–414; `brooks_ranges` p.71). Measured moves guide profit-taking; they are not entry signals and must not be faded on their own (`brooks_ranges` p.72).
- **Evidence:** moderate

### 14 — Equilibrium chop: WAIT (no trade all session)
- **Setup:** Asia and early London both build value in the same 8-dollar band; H1 closes alternate above and below the H1 20-EMA seven times; H1 rotation factor for the session is +1.
- **Decision:** WAIT — no entries in either direction until an H1 close outside the band that holds on retest.
- **Invalidation of the wait:** balance-edge acceptance as in #8.
- **Why:** markets near an intermediate moving average are close to random walk, and the absence of imbalance nearly guarantees no edge (`grimes_art_science` p.209); a rotation factor near zero says neither side is trying (`dalton_mind_over_markets` p.133); Brooks's advice inside a tight range is the same — wait for the breakout or the failed breakout (`brooks_reversals` p.331).
- **Evidence:** moderate

### 15 — Very small opening range: breakout mode, direction not pre-committed
- **Setup:** London's first 60 minutes span only 3,346–3,350, about 20% of the mean session range of the last ten sessions. Bodies overlap heavily; the M15 20-EMA is flat.
- **Decision:** WAIT with two armed conditions — long on an M15 close above 3,350 that holds, short on an M15 close below 3,346 that holds. Do not form a directional opinion in advance.
- **Invalidation:** an armed side triggers and then closes back inside within three bars ⇒ disarm that side and expect the opposite edge to break (per example #9).
- **Why:** an opening range under about a quarter of recent session range makes traders enter on a break in either direction, and occasionally produces a trend-from-the-open session (`brooks_reversals` p.413). This is breakout mode: a break either way is expected to follow through, so direction must not be pre-committed (`brooks_ranges` p.11).
- **Evidence:** moderate

### 16 — Role reversal that does not appear (the level was never there)
- **Setup:** An H1 level at 3,344 scored 2 and was decisively broken to the downside two sessions earlier. Price now returns to 3,344 during the overlap and simply trades straight through it — no wick rejection, no range contraction, no change in bar size or tick volume.
- **Decision:** SKIP; cancel the role-reversal short and remove the level from the active set.
- **Invalidation:** none — the absence of a reaction is itself the signal.
- **Why:** Grimes names this the third possible outcome at a level, the one most implementations forget to plan for: no action at all, meaning the level was simply not there (`grimes_art_science` p.134). He also gives the one timeframe-independent test in the corpus — if significant action is expected at a specific price, it must actually occur at that price; its absence is information regardless of which chart you are watching (`grimes_art_science` p.149).
- **Evidence:** moderate — this is the second failure mode in this file, and unlike most it is falsifiable in real time.

### 17 — Excess level decaying on a second test
- **Setup:** NY session prints a long lower tail to 3,328 (excess, `level_score` +1). Price returns to 3,331 later the same session and holds — first probe respected. Twenty minutes later it returns a third time and spends two consecutive M15 bars trading between 3,328 and 3,331.
- **Decision:** On the first return, OPEN long with invalidation below 3,328. On the third return, SKIP — reduce the level's score and stand aside.
- **Invalidation:** M15 close below 3,328 at any point.
- **Why:** a spike or tail extreme is a reliable reference only for the first probe into it; repeated tests within the same interval establish acceptance inside the excess and negate it as a reference (`dalton_mind_over_markets` p.252).
- **Evidence:** weak — Dalton states the mechanism for TPO profiles; the M15-bar translation is ours.

### 18 — Rotation factor disagreeing with always-in (WAIT)
- **Setup:** M15 `always_in` is long after two strong bull bars off the London low. But the session's M30 rotation tally is −4: highs have been stepping down all session even though lows are rising. Price sits mid-range between the Asia high and the London low.
- **Decision:** WAIT. Do not take the with-trend long swing; at most consider a scalp back toward the nearest magnet.
- **Invalidation of the wait:** an M30 interval that prints both a higher high and a higher low, turning the tally toward zero, plus an M15 close beyond the session's upper edge.
- **Why:** the rotation factor answers only "which way is it trying to go", and a strongly negative tally against a fresh long flip says the attempted direction and the recent impulse disagree — the definition of a two-sided, uncertain market (`dalton_mind_over_markets` p.133; `brooks_ranges` p.133). Compressing highs with rising lows is a converging range, which resolves ambiguously.
- **Evidence:** weak — the conjunction rule (require sign agreement) is our construction; Dalton uses the rotation factor alone.

## Learning outcome

After this topic the model must, from closed bars alone, emit for each timeframe: the pivot set at the requested order, a trend label drawn from `{up, down, uncertain, transition}`, an always-in direction with the specific closed bars that set it, and the nearest scored support/resistance zones with their significance evidence. It must then classify any move through a level as one of `breakout / breakout pullback / breakout test / failed breakout / failed failure / non-event`, naming the owning timeframe and the exact breakout point, and label the resulting trade idea as initiative or responsive relative to the prior session's value proxy. It must refuse to trade — outputting WAIT or SKIP with a reason — when the level fails the significance score, when structure is `uncertain`, when price is rotating around an intermediate moving average, or when the only argument for the trade is a favourable reward/risk geometry at an unvalidated level.

## Implementation

All fields computable from closed OHLCV bars plus the UTC session clock unless flagged.

| Field | Computation | Timeframe | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| `pivot_high_1[tf]` | bar `i` where `high[i] > high[i-1]` and `high[i] > high[i+1]`; confirmed only after bar `i+1` closes | all | Strict Grimes rule (`grimes p.32`). Emit with a one-bar confirmation lag. Ties are *not* pivots. |
| `pivot_high_1_brooks[tf]` | same with `>=` on both sides | all | Second, looser series for Brooks-style swing counting (`brooks_ranges p.14`). Keep both; never mix them in one rule. |
| `pivot_order_n[tf]` | recursive: an order-n pivot high is an order-(n−1) pivot high flanked by lower order-(n−1) pivot highs | all | Handle runs of ≥3 same-side pivots by anchoring to the extreme of the run (`grimes pp.33–35`). |
| `swing_seq[tf]` | ordered list of (order-2 pivots) with HH/HL/LH/LL tags | H4, H1, M15 | Feed the trend label; do not build from order-1 pivots. |
| `trend_label[tf]` | `up` if last two order-2 pivot highs and lows both rising; `down` if both falling; `transition` if one HH and one LL in the same leg; else `uncertain` | H4, H1, M15 | Four-state, not binary (`grimes p.96`). |
| `complex_pullback_flag[tf]` | in `up`, when a trend-change condition fires, check whether the current countertrend leg length is within ±35% of the prior countertrend leg and terminates within 1×ATR of it | H1, H4 | Guard against the Dow-rule trap (`grimes p.97`). Threshold is a bot heuristic, not from the book — tune it. |
| `always_in[tf]` | flip to long after 2 consecutive closed bars with body ≥60% of range, both bullish, the second closing beyond the first; mirror for short | H1, M15 | Fallback chain when no flip: closes vs 20-EMA, then direction of the move into congestion (`brooks_reversals pp.329, 333`). Body/range threshold is our operationalisation of "strong trend bar". |
| `breakout_event` | closed bar whose close exceeds a registered level zone's far edge | per level's own tf | Store `{level_id, tf, breakout_point, bar_time, direction}`. Wick-only excursions are not breakouts. |
| `bars_outside` | count of consecutive closed bars whose close is beyond `breakout_point` | breakout tf | Drives the failure filter. |
| `failed_breakout` | `bars_outside <= 3` AND a subsequent closed bar closes back inside with body ≥50% of range against the breakout | breakout tf | Grimes's 2–3 bar rule of thumb plus a momentum requirement (`grimes p.147`). |
| `breakout_pullback` | retrace of 1–5 bars after `breakout_event` that does not satisfy `failed_breakout` | breakout tf | (`brooks_ranges p.11`) |
| `breakout_test` | `breakout_pullback` whose extreme comes within `test_band` of `breakout_point` | breakout tf | `test_band` = 0.15 × ATR(14) on that tf. May occur 20+ bars later — keep the breakout point registered until invalidated. |
| `breakout_gap` | `min(low of pullback) − breakout_point` for a bull break | breakout tf | Negative value allowed ("negative gap"); flag such measured-move projections as low confidence (`brooks_ranges p.76`). |
| `failed_failure` | after `failed_breakout`, a closed bar trading beyond the high (bull case) of a prior bar within 5 bars, back in the original direction | breakout tf | (`brooks_ranges p.12`) |
| `level_zone` | `{price, half_width}` with `half_width` = 0.25 × ATR(14) of the level's owning tf | per level | Enforces zone-not-line (`grimes p.115`). Replaces the v1.2 fixed "4–5 units on H4". |
| `level_score` | +1 each for: origin is H4/D1 order-2 pivot; ≥3 prior touches; coincides with a tail/spike extreme; prior-session or prior-day H/L; round number (multiple of 10 or 25 for XAUUSD) | per level | Require score ≥2 to trade (`grimes p.119`; `murphy p.71`; round numbers `murphy pp.74–75`, which uses gold as its worked example). |
| `level_recency` | bars since last touch, normalised | per level | Murphy's third significance factor (`murphy p.71`). |
| `decisive_break` | close beyond the zone by ≥ max(0.5 × ATR(14), 0.3% of price) and held for 2 closes | level's tf | Operationalises Murphy's 1–3% benchmark scaled to intraday gold; 3% is far too wide for M15 (`murphy p.74`). |
| `role_reversal_zone` | after `decisive_break`, re-register the zone with inverted polarity | level's tf | Expires if price closes back through it (`murphy pp.72–74`). |
| `measured_move_spike` | `breakout_point + (breakout_point − spike_start)` | breakout tf | Primary partial-target (`brooks_ranges p.71`). |
| `measured_move_range` | `breakout_point ± range_height` | breakout tf | (`brooks_ranges p.76`) |
| `measured_move_gap` | `2 × gap_midpoint − leg_start` | breakout tf | Only when `breakout_gap > 0`. |
| `magnet_list` | ranked list of: measured-move targets, prior-session H/L/open/close, opening-range edges, unfilled gaps, large-trend-bar opposite extremes, round numbers | all | (`brooks_ranges p.81`) Used for target selection and for suppressing counter-entries before the magnet is reached. |
| `session_range[s]` | high/low of the completed session `s` | session | Asia 00–07, London 08–13, overlap 13–16, NY 16–21 UTC. |
| `value_proxy[s]` | narrowest contiguous price band containing 70% of `tick_volume` from M1 bars in session `s` | session | **Approximation.** True Market Profile uses TPO/time or real volume; MT5 `tick_volume` is a tick-count proxy for spot gold. Flag confidence as reduced. If tick volume is unavailable, fall back to the 70% band by *time* (M1 bar count per price bucket), which is closer to Dalton's original TPO construction. |
| `open_type` | classify the session open vs `session_range[prev]` and `value_proxy[prev]`: `drive` (no return through the open in the first 4 M15 bars), `test_drive` (probe beyond a registered reference, fail, reverse through the open), `auction_in_range`, `auction_out_of_range` | M15 | (`dalton pp.83–92`) `drive`/`test_drive` need the "no return through the open" test, which is closed-bar computable. |
| `initiative_responsive` | buy at/above `value_proxy[prev]` ⇒ initiative; buy below ⇒ responsive; mirrored for sells | session | (`dalton p.66`) |
| `balance_flag` | ≥2 consecutive sessions whose `value_proxy` bands overlap by ≥60% | session/D1 | Dalton uses "several days of overlapping value" without a fixed number (`dalton pp.87, 197`); the 60%/2-session threshold is ours. |
| `excess_tail` | bar where tail length ≥ 2 × body and the tail extends beyond the prior session's range | M15, H1 | Register the tail extreme as a level with `level_score` +1 (`dalton pp.130–131`). Downgrade after price spends a second M15 bar inside it. |
| `rotation_factor[s]` | per 30-min interval within the session: +1 if higher high else −1 (0 if equal), same for lows; sum both series | M30 aggregated | (`dalton p.133`) Objective directional tally; require sign agreement with `always_in` for with-trend swings. |
| `opening_range[s]` | high/low of the larger of (a) the first congestion or (b) the largest leg of the first 30–90 minutes of the session | M5/M15 | Ambiguous by construction — Brooks says there is rarely agreement on its exact bounds (`brooks_reversals p.413`). Emit both the 30-min and 60-min variants and treat disagreement as reduced confidence. |
| `opening_range_class` | ratio of `opening_range` height to the mean session range of the last 10 sessions: `<25%`, `33–50%`, `>50%` | session | Drives expectation: very small ⇒ breakout in either direction; mid ⇒ expect range to grow toward an average session and a measured move off the OR (`brooks_reversals pp.413–414`). |
| `equilibrium_flag` | ≥6 of the last 10 closes alternate sides of the 20-EMA on the decision tf | H1, M15 | Hard stand-aside gate (`grimes p.209`). |
| `retracement_pct` | `(leg_extreme − current) / leg_height` | all | Report bands 33/50/66 for context only; do not trigger on them (`murphy pp.94–95`). |
| `rule_eval.R` | realised P&L ÷ initial risk per trade | trade log | Required before any rule here is promoted above `weak`: t-test the mean R against zero (`grimes p.409`). |

### Mapping single-open concepts onto a 24-hour instrument

Brooks's opening range and Dalton's open types were both written for an instrument with one meaningful open per day. XAUUSD has three, plus a daily rollover, and the corpus gives no guidance on the translation. The mapping below is this bot's construction and should be labelled as such wherever it drives a decision.

| Book concept | Original referent | XAUUSD mapping | Confidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opening range | First 30–90 minutes of the RTH session (`brooks_reversals` p.413) | Computed separately for London (08:00–09:00/09:30 UTC) and NY (16:00–17:00 UTC). Asia gets a range but is treated as a balance proxy, not an opening range, because it usually lacks a directional open. | moderate |
| "Yesterday's always-in carries into today's open" (`brooks_reversals` p.369) | Prior RTH close → next RTH open | Prior *session's* closing always-in carries into the next session's open, with the strongest carry across the Asia→London handover and the weakest across the NY→Asia handover. | weak |
| Previous day's value area (`dalton_mind_over_markets` p.66) | Prior full pit session | Two references, both maintained: the prior same-session value proxy (London vs London) and the prior D1 value proxy. When they conflict, the D1 reference governs the initiative/responsive label and the session reference governs the open-type label. | weak |
| Open-Drive (`dalton_mind_over_markets` p.83) | Aggressive auction from the bell with no return through the open | First four M15 bars of a session never trade back through the session's opening price. Directly computable on closed bars. | moderate |
| Open-Test-Drive (`dalton_mind_over_markets` p.85) | Probe a known reference, fail, reverse through the open | Probe beyond a registered `level_score >= 2` reference (typically the prior session's extreme) within the first hour, no acceptance, then a close back through the session open. | moderate |
| Open-Auction in / out of range (`dalton_mind_over_markets` pp.90–92) | Open inside / outside prior day's range | Open inside / outside the *prior session's* range, with the prior-day range as a secondary tag. Inside ⇒ estimate this session's span from the prior session's span (`dalton_mind_over_markets` p.96). | moderate |
| Report-time stand-aside (`brooks_reversals` pp.369–370) | 7:00 a.m. PST US releases | 12:30 and 14:00 UTC US releases, plus 08:00–08:30 UTC European data. Suppress new entries for the first two M5 bars after the timestamp. | moderate |

**Data the bot may not have:** true traded volume (spot gold is OTC — only tick counts), real Market Profile TPO structure, order-book depth. Every Dalton-derived field above is therefore a proxy and must be labelled as such in the model's reasoning output.

## Contradictions between sources

| Author A position | Author B position | How to resolve for this bot |
|---|---|---|
| **Grimes:** most support/resistance levels traders use are indistinguishable from randomly drawn lines; he demonstrates this by hiding the bars, drawing random lines, and producing textbook holds, breaks and role reversals (`grimes pp.117–119`). | **Murphy:** support and resistance are real, arise from identifiable participant psychology, and reliably reverse roles after a significant penetration (`murphy pp.69–74`). | Grimes wins on method — his is the only reproducible test in the corpus. Keep Murphy's *mechanism* as the reason a level might matter, but gate every level behind `level_score >= 2`. Unscored levels are logged, never traded. This is the single most important gate in the topic. |
| **Brooks:** inside a trading range roughly 80% of breakout attempts fail, so the default is to fade the edges (`brooks_ranges p.134`). **Grimes:** breakout failures are more common than successful breakouts (`grimes p.163`). | **Dalton:** a balance-area breakout is a trade you "almost have to do" — go with the break, minimal risk, high potential (`dalton pp.203–204`). | Not a true contradiction once the reference frame is fixed, and the resolution is mechanical: Brooks/Grimes are describing breakouts of the *day-timeframe* range with no acceptance test; Dalton requires *acceptance* outside a multi-session balance and exits the moment price re-enters. Rule: fade unaccepted breaks of intraday range edges; go with breaks of `balance_flag` regions **only after a second close outside**, and exit on re-entry. Never fade and follow the same break. |
| **Grimes:** the strict Dow HH/HL labelling system will flag a downtrend at exactly the point you should be buying, because complex consolidations are common (`grimes p.97`). | **Murphy / classical Dow:** the failure at point 5 plus a violation of point 4 *constitutes* a downside reversal (`murphy p.69`). | Implement Murphy's labeller, then run Grimes's complex-pullback guard on top. Emit `transition`, not `down`, until the second countertrend leg is clearly longer than the first. |
| **Grimes:** a pivot requires a *strictly* higher high than both neighbours (`grimes p.32`). | **Brooks:** a swing high's high need only be *at or above* both neighbours (`brooks_ranges p.14`). | Both series are computed and kept separate. Use Grimes's strict series for regime labelling (fewer, cleaner pivots) and Brooks's permissive series for bar-counting setups. Mixing them silently is how the v1.2 draft produced inconsistent swing sets. |
| **Brooks:** the market always has an always-in direction; a trader could theoretically stay positioned all day, reversing at each signal (`brooks_reversals pp.328–329`). | **Grimes:** the third trend state, `uncertain`, is essential; forcing structure into a pattern is a Procrustean error, and markets in equilibrium offer no edge at all (`grimes pp.96, 209`). | Keep `always_in` as a *bias* field, since it is genuinely always defined, but gate execution on Grimes's `uncertain` / `equilibrium_flag`. The bot may hold an opinion in a range; it may not trade on that opinion alone. |
| **Brooks:** magnets pull price toward them, so while a magnet is within reach trade only in its direction (`brooks_ranges p.66`). | **Grimes:** in a random-walk environment, a large target relative to a tight stop yields exactly zero expectancy; you need an independent non-random reason (`grimes pp.119–120`). | Use magnets for *exit* and target selection only. A magnet is never sufficient reason to enter, and never justifies a counter-entry at the magnet without a separate structural signal. |
| **Dalton:** excess (a tail or gap) should serve as future support/resistance because the same participants should act again there (`dalton p.131`). | **Dalton himself, later:** a spike extreme is a valid reference only for the *first* probe into it; repeated tests within the same interval mean price will likely go through (`dalton p.252`). | Register excess levels with a decay: full weight on the first retest, `level_score` reduced by 1 on the second, removed after price closes a full interval inside the excess region. |
| **Brooks:** a breakout pullback that violates the breakout point is still likely a pullback, not a failure (`brooks_ranges p.53`); **Grimes** agrees the level need not hold cleanly (`grimes p.146`). | **Dalton:** on a balance breakout, re-entry into the balance area is itself the rejection signal and shorts/longs should be exited (`dalton p.203`). | Scope by structure size. For a single-level breakout, allow the pullback to violate the level and require a *momentum close* against the break before calling failure. For a multi-session balance breakout, use Dalton's tighter rule — the balance edge is a much stronger reference and re-entry is meaningful. |

## Gaps

Nothing in this corpus supplies the following, all of which this topic needs:

1. **Any measured base rate for XAUUSD.** Brooks's "80% of range breakouts fail" and "80% of reversals fail" are assertions about the E-mini in 2011, stated without data (`brooks_ranges p.134`; `brooks_reversals p.331`). Grimes says breakout failures outnumber successes but gives no figure (`grimes p.163`). Gold's intraday behaviour — particularly around the 08:00 and 13:00 UTC handovers — may differ substantially.
2. **A validated significance threshold for levels.** Grimes proves unqualified levels are indistinguishable from random but does not supply a passing test. The `level_score >= 2` gate above is a construction, not a finding. The corpus contains no method for demonstrating that a *specific* level set beats random lines — that experiment has to be run on XAUUSD tick history.
3. **Session-aware structure for a 24-hour OTC instrument.** Brooks's opening range and Dalton's open types are built around a single daily pit/RTH open. Gold has three meaningful opens per day plus a daily rollover, and the corpus is silent on how prior-session value should be weighted when three sessions compete.
4. **Real volume.** Every Dalton concept in this file — value area, initiative/responsive, excess, balance — is defined on time or traded volume. Spot gold offers neither; tick count is a proxy of unknown fidelity, and no book here discusses the substitution.
5. **Quantified interaction between timeframes.** Everyone states that structure is timeframe-specific; nobody specifies how much an H4 `down` label should discount an M15 long. The corpus offers only "beware of patterns contradicted by higher timeframes" (`grimes p.239`).
6. **Decay rates.** How long a breakout point, an excess tail, or a role-reversed zone stays live is left entirely to judgement. Brooks says a breakout test may come 20+ bars later (`brooks_ranges p.53`), which is a lower bound, not a rule.
7. **News-driven structure.** Brooks explicitly says to sit out the minutes after a report because algorithmic participants have a speed edge (`brooks_reversals pp.369–370`), but no source explains how to reinterpret levels broken by a news spike — precisely the dominant failure mode for gold around 12:30 and 14:00 UTC releases.
